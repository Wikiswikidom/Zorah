# Zorah Competitive Research — 2026

## Research scope

Reviewed current handbag/leather ecommerce examples and current ecommerce UX research to inform Zorah's Phase 2 direction.

### Primary references
- Polène — product-first minimalism, restrained navigation and editorial product presentation.
- Loewe — deep handbag taxonomy, material-led product language, multiple carry styles and strong product imagery.
- Strathberry — editorial campaigns, product families, bestsellers and craftsmanship storytelling.
- Zashadu — Lagos-made identity, chapter storytelling, reclaimed leather, scarcity and journal content.
- Aspinal of London — personalization, leather education and gifting.
- DeMellier — timeless product positioning and story-led commerce.
- Baymard — current product-page, search, category and mobile ecommerce UX research.

## Findings

### 1. Product discovery must be effortless

Zorah needs clear product categories, predictable labels, search, filters and a strong mobile menu rather than a visually clever but confusing navigation system. Baymard's current search research shows that many ecommerce sites still fail common search needs, so Zorah should treat search as a first-class feature rather than a footer utility.

### 2. Product pages are the decision centre

Baymard's 2026 product-page research reinforces that users rely heavily on product pages before purchasing. For handbags specifically, Zorah should show multiple high-quality angles, an in-scale/worn image, dimensions/capacity, materials, care, availability, delivery/returns and an obvious Add to Bag action.

### 3. Editorial storytelling should support commerce

The strongest handbag brands combine campaign imagery and shopping access. Zorah should use editorial sections to build desire, then give the shopper a direct route to a collection or product.

### 4. Craft is a conversion asset

Leather texture, construction, hardware and maker/process stories should be treated as product evidence. Zorah should show the work rather than merely saying "handcrafted".

### 5. Lagos should be visible in the brand story

Zashadu demonstrates that Lagos craftsmanship can be a luxury differentiator rather than something hidden. Zorah should own its Nigerian origin and present it with confidence and sophistication.

### 6. Personalization can increase product value

Personalization and custom orders should be a guided consultation, not a generic contact form. Zorah's Custom Orders pathway therefore has its own first-class page and will later connect to an admin workflow.

### 7. Mobile is not a reduced desktop experience

Product pages need swipeable galleries, large touch targets, persistent purchase access, readable product information and mobile-friendly filters. The mobile journey should be designed as a primary journey.

### 8. Performance is part of luxury

A slow site makes a premium brand feel cheap. Zorah should use optimized imagery, restrained animation, minimal JavaScript and fast transitions. Motion should communicate state or hierarchy, never delay shopping.

### 9. Merchandising needs both discovery and urgency

Large marketplaces prove the value of visible sale/offer destinations, while luxury brands demonstrate that promotional content can remain restrained. Zorah's admin system should support campaign placements, scheduled dates, truthful countdowns, product rails and editorial promotion without turning the storefront into a noisy discount site.

## Design conclusion

Zorah should not attempt to look like a clone of a European luxury house. The winning position is **quiet luxury + Lagos craft + modern editorial commerce**.

The visual system should therefore be mostly ivory/black with leather brown, controlled deep green and small muted-gold details. Typography and photography should carry the premium feeling; animation should remain subtle and purposeful.
